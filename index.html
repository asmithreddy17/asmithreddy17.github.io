The Problem

Financial advisors managing $10M+ institutional portfolios spent 40+ minutes per session navigating a 9-tab analytics platform. Risk alerts that required action were buried 4 levels deep. The product had accumulated features without restructuring the information model to match how advisors actually worked.

The tempting diagnosis was "too many features, too little UX polish." The correct diagnosis, which only emerged from the research, was different: the interface was organized around internal product team boundaries, not around the tasks advisors needed to complete in a session.

Research & Discovery

I ran 12 structured interviews with financial advisors and compliance officers — not surveys, not analytics pulls, actual sessions where I watched people use the product and asked them to narrate their decisions. Three consistent findings emerged that changed my understanding of the problem:

  • Advisors didn't navigate linearly. They arrived at the platform with a specific task (check a risk alert, review a portfolio, execute a trade), and the 9-tab navigation forced them to remember which tab held which function instead of surfacing the relevant data for the task they had.
  • Risk alerts were the highest-priority action in any session, but they were buried behind two non-obvious navigation steps. Advisors had built workarounds — bookmarks to specific sub-pages — that engineering didn't know about.
  • The information density on the portfolio overview screen was not the problem. Advisors wanted the data; they just wanted it organized by what they needed to act on first, not by data category.
The Product Decisions

The research finding was that information sequence was the problem, not information volume. That reframing changed every subsequent decision.

  • Restructure from 9 tabs to 4 workflow-based sections — Dashboard (portfolio overview + live risk alerts), Trade & Orders, Clients & Reports, Settings. Navigation organized around what advisors do, not what the product contains.
  • Promote risk alerts to the primary dashboard surface — the highest-urgency action in any session needed zero navigation to reach. Surfacing it on load reduced the time-to-action on compliance-critical alerts from minutes to seconds.
  • Preserve information density, restructure hierarchy — research showed advisors wanted the data; the decision was not to reduce it but to sequence it. Portfolio performance anchored the view; deeper analysis available on demand, not imposed.
  • Design system components extended for the data table and alert patterns — adopted across 3 other Infosys product teams; the component investment compounded across the organization, not just this product.
Stakeholder & Engineering Alignment

Restructuring navigation from 9 tabs to 4 workflows was not a small ask for a product with 3 compliance officers and 8 engineers already aligned to the existing IA. I presented the research findings with specificity — not "users are confused" but "advisors have built bookmark workarounds for sub-pages because the navigation doesn't match their task model, and here is the session data." When the problem was grounded in what users actually did, the structural change became the obvious solution rather than a design preference.

Engineering collaboration continued through build — I reviewed live implementations against intent, flagged inconsistencies before they reached users, and iterated on component behavior until the shipped product matched the research-driven decision. Post-launch design debt was close to zero.

The Problem I Chose to Solve

Finance-curious professionals and heavy news readers used 4–5 separate apps every morning — Bloomberg for market data, BBC for headlines, YouTube for CNBC commentary, a separate stocks app. The content existed. The problem was fragmentation: every context switch cost attention and time. A competitive audit confirmed no product had consolidated live market data, multi-publication editorial news, and live video in one coherent interface.

The opportunity was a high-value, underserved user: finance-adjacent professionals priced out of Bloomberg ($24K/year), willing to pay for consolidation, with long daily sessions and strong retention characteristics.

Discovery & Research

I audited five products (Bloomberg, Apple News, Yahoo Finance, Feedly, YouTube) to understand why the gap existed and what each had failed to solve. The finding: every product solved one pillar (data, or news, or video) and treated the others as secondary. No one had designed for the user who needed all three in a single working session.

I defined three user personas from behavior, not demographics: Finance-Curious Professionals (want market performance + top headlines, no terminal), Heavy News Readers (multi-publication, don't want five subscriptions), and Passive Monitors (live news as ambient background). Marcus, 31, Finance Operations Analyst — 25–35 minutes per morning across four apps — became the anchor persona.

The Product Decision That Changed Everything

V1 gave equal visual weight to news, markets, and video — treating the three content pillars as peers. I ran a 6-person informal usability study with colleagues who fit the target persona. The finding was immediate: "It feels like three apps on one screen." No one knew where to start. Equal weight had eliminated the hierarchy the product needed to be usable.

I made the call to kill the equal-weight architecture before committing to engineering. The rebuild established a clear priority stack: markets anchor the top (persistent, always visible), news fills the primary content area, video occupies a smaller secondary panel. That hierarchy was the product decision. Everything else was execution.

  • Markets pinned top — users who track indices check compulsively; making them navigate to find the S&P would cause churn to a dedicated finance app within a week.
  • Publication-based grouping over algorithm — algorithmic feeds obscure editorial voice; users calibrate trust per topic per publication, and removing that signal reduces comprehension quality.
  • Video sized as secondary panel, not primary — the passive monitoring use case means users run PULSE in the background, not as the primary screen; a fullscreen default would compete with the reading workflow it was meant to support.
  • 3-proxy fallback chain for RSS (rss2json → allorigins → corsproxy) — infrastructure decision to guarantee the "never empty" experience promise; one failing proxy silently hands off to the next.

Market Opportunity & Strategy

Immigrants and international professionals are disproportionately underserved by mainstream banking apps. They rely on 3–4 separate products to cover cross-border transfers, local bill payments, and day-to-day spending — each with its own fees, onboarding friction, and incomplete view of their finances. A competitive audit across Venmo, Wise, Cash App, and Revolut confirmed no single product served this user as a first-class customer.

The product strategy: trust-first, cross-border-native, consolidated. Not a feature-level differentiation from Venmo or Wise, but a positioning decision to serve a segment those products treat as an edge case. The addressable market — approximately 47M immigrants in the US (U.S. Census Bureau, 2023), high-income international professionals, students on financial aid from abroad — was both high-value and systematically underserved.

Discovery & Research

I interviewed 8 users who send money internationally at least once a month. Core pain points: hidden fees revealed only at the confirmation screen (after the user had committed to the transfer mentally), 7+ steps per core action, and exchange rate displays that required mental math to understand actual cost. I benchmarked Wise (international transfer UX), Venmo (P2P interaction patterns), PhonePe (South Asian immigrant segment), and Zelle (domestic speed, no international support) to identify which patterns to borrow and which gaps required original work.

Key Product Decisions
  • Trust over speed in the confirmation flow — original v1 was minimal (one tap to confirm). Testing showed users hesitated even when the steps were simple. The problem was trust, not familiarity. I added a transaction summary step — recipient name, exact amount in their currency, fee — before the final confirm. One extra tap for measurably higher confidence and completion.
  • Fee transparency pulled forward to entry, not confirmation — users who saw the fee at the end felt deceived even when the fee was reasonable. Moving disclosure to the amount entry screen removed the "hidden fee" perception that was the single biggest trust failure in the research.
  • Home screen stripped to three elements — first draft surfaced everything (spending, savings, bills, history) on load. Testing showed this paralyzed users with multiple accounts. Stripped to: balance, recent transactions, quick-action shortcuts. Depth on demand, not imposed.
  • v3 driven entirely by competitive audit — audited Venmo, Cash App, Chime, PhonePe and identified 6 UX gaps the market hadn't addressed: no spending visibility, missing bill reminders, no savings tracking, too many taps for frequent contacts, no financial health overview, poor transaction search. v3 addressed all six in one release cycle.
